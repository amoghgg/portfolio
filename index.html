Performance as a stress test for machine perception.

Research statement

Machines are learning to watch us: to track a hand, read a face, compress a body and draw it again on the other side of a call. Most of that technology is tuned for people sitting still, evenly lit, doing ordinary things. Performance is the opposite. Every eyebrow and finger carries meaning, the light is deliberately hostile, and one wrong read ruins the moment.

I use performance as a stress test, to find where machine perception breaks, who it breaks for, and how to build systems that hold. Lights asks whether a camera can take a lighting cue from a performer's hand when a single false trigger blacks out a live show. SignFlip asks what happens to sign language when generative video redraws the signer, where a lost eyebrow raise turns a question into a statement. The live pieces below ask the same question for an audience of one.

About

Noida, India

The company

I came to engineering through theatre. Rehearsal rooms taught me how bodies move through space, how timing carries emotion, and how a small gesture can change what a scene means. That way of thinking never left. It moved into the systems I build.

By day I'm an Assistant Product Manager at Innovatiview, where I design and ship identity-verification systems (face matching, liveness, on-site enrolment) used to verify staff at national examinations in India. So I know what machine perception does at scale, in bad light, on cheap phones, when it matters.
