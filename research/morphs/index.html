Morphs

One passport photo made from two people's faces, so that it matches both of them. Can you catch that from the photo alone?

Status
First pass, not published
Data
10,042 consented research images
Measured with
ISO/IEC 30107-3 error rates

The attack

A morph is a face built to pass as two people.

Two people blend their faces into one photo and put it on a passport or an ID. Face matching then accepts the photo for either of them, so two people can share one document. Border agencies call this a morphing attack. The hard version is catching it from the one photo on the document, with no second picture to compare against.

Why this one is about honesty

This is a field where papers report 99% and the number falls apart the moment it meets real photos. So I built the project around rules that make flattering results hard to get. No person appears on both the training and the test side, not even as half of a morph. The detector is tested on morphing tools it never saw in training. I wrote the scoring code and its tests before any model existed. And a modern neural network has to beat a 1990s texture method, or I say it didn't.

Three bugs caught by distrusting good news

  1. The 98% that wasn't. My first error rate was 1.6%. That was too good, so I went looking. 3,532 images from the "unseen" morphing tools had quietly slipped into training. Nobody overlapped, so my checks stayed quiet. What gave it away: every tool scored equally well, and generalisation never looks like that.
  2. Reading the frame, not the face. The texture method was partly spotting warp marks near the image edge. Cropping to the face roughly doubled the error. The honest number is the worse one.
  3. Two people called "001". Two datasets numbered their people the same way, so different people would have counted as one. I caught it before it did any damage.

What survived

On clean photos the neural network only ties the 1990s method. Under compression too light to see, it gets measurably worse. Below that, both fall apart. An earlier single run seemed to show the network pulling ahead on badly damaged photos. That went away once I ran three seeds, so I withdrew it.

My guess at why: the source photos are 1350 pixels wide and both detectors see 224. That throws away 97% of the pixels, and the fine skin texture where a morph shows is what goes first. That's the next experiment.

Everything here is a first pass on stand-in data, with simulated print-and-scan. Nothing is published.
