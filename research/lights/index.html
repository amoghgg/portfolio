Lights

Calling the lighting cues in a play with your hands. A camera watches from the wings, and a mistake blacks out the stage in front of an audience.

Status
Paper written, not yet published
With
Varun Tiwari, Manipal University Jaipur
Prototype
lights.amoghbajpai.com
Code
github.com/amoghgg/lights

The problem

A cue that is correct in content and late in delivery has failed.

In small theatre, the person who knows when a cue belongs and the person who can actually fire it are almost never the same. An assistant director watches from the wings with nothing to press. A volunteer runs the lighting desk off a cue sheet. So every cue has to travel between them: seen, signalled, understood, found on the desk, pressed. Each handover costs time, and the lights land late, night after night.

The idea

The five cues

The system reads five cues from bare hands. There are no markers, no gloves, and nothing to calibrate. Each cue gets a timing contract. A blackout has to land within a fifth of a second, so it fires the moment it is recognised, and the gesture is made hard to do by accident instead. A colour change can wait, so it only gets lined up, and fires when you confirm it. The master level moves through every value on its way, so it can't be confirmed. It works like a fader: nothing moves until you take hold of it.

Hover a cue and the lights do it to him.

CueGestureContract
BlackoutBoth hands, open to fistInstant · two hands
RestoreBoth hands, fist to openInstant · two hands
Area specialBoth palms held toward an areaConfirmed · two hands
MasterOpen hand moving up or downHeld, like a fader · one hand
Colour stateOne hand sweeping sidewaysConfirmed · one hand

The first version used a double clap for blackout. It never worked, because the camera loses both hands at the exact moment they meet. Any gesture that is invisible at its own climax has the same problem. The master used to need a palm facing down, which meant looking at your own hand, and the whole point is that your eyes stay on the stage.

What we measure

Papers on gesture recognition routinely report accuracy in the high nineties. Those numbers come from short clips cut to contain a gesture in the first place, so they can't measure false firing at all. On a stage the two errors are nothing alike: a missed cue just goes back to the old way, but a false one blacks out the stage in front of an audience.

So the headline number is false cues per hour. People wear the system for hours while doing ordinary work in the wings, like handling a script, talking with their hands or standing still, and every cue it fires is a mistake. Next to that, we time how long a cue takes to land against the usual relay to a desk operator. Several versions of the detector run through the same loop, from plain rules to a small learned network, and everything is split by person rather than by clip.

The paper is written. Results will go here once it is out.
