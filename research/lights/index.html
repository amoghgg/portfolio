Lights

Markerless gestural cue triggering for theatrical lighting. A hand in the wings calls the cue, and the camera has to be right when a mistake blacks out the stage.

The problem

A cue that is correct in content and late in delivery has failed.

In small, under-resourced theatre, the person who knows when a cue belongs and the person who can execute it are rarely the same. An assistant director watches from the wings with no control surface; a volunteer works the lighting desk from a cue sheet. Every cue has to travel between them: perceived, signalled, understood, found on the desk, executed. Each handoff costs time, and transitions land late, night after night.

The idea

Cue vocabulary

The prototype recognises five cues from hand landmarks alone, with no markers, gloves or calibration. The vocabulary is deliberately small and designed so the gestures are structurally hard to confuse: static and dynamic, one hand and two.

What we measure

Gesture-recognition papers routinely report accuracies in the nineties. Those numbers come from pre-segmented clips that contain a gesture by construction, so they can't measure false activation at all. On a stage the errors are asymmetric: a missed cue merely fails to help, but a false one blacks out the stage in front of an audience.

So the headline number is false triggers per hour, alongside cue delivery latency against a manual baseline. Cues are grouped by timing contract: timing-critical cues commit as soon as they are recognised, while selective ones wait behind an explicit confirmation. Three detectors run on identical data through an identical streaming loop: naive rules, rules with structural fixes, and a small causal temporal network. Evaluation is split by participant, not by clip.

Results aren't published yet. This page will be updated when the evaluation is complete.
